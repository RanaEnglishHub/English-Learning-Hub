Active & Passive Voice

Active: The subject does the action. Passive: The subject receives the action.

ACTIVE

The students clean the garden.

Doer → verb → receiver
PASSIVE

The garden is cleaned by the students.

Receiver → be + past participle → optional doer
TenseActivePassive
Present simpleThey make posters.Posters are made.
Past simpleThey made posters.Posters were made.
Present continuousThey are making posters.Posters are being made.
Present perfectThey have made posters.Posters have been made.
Future with willThey will make posters.Posters will be made.
ModalThey must make posters.Posters must be made.
Use passive voice when the action or result matters more than the doer, or when the doer is unknown. Only verbs that take an object can normally form a passive.

Direct & Reported Speech

DIRECT

Hessa said, “I am ready.”

REPORTED

Hessa said (that) she was ready.

When reporting later using a past reporting verb, verb forms often shift back in time:

DirectOften reported as
am/is →was
are →were
present simple →past simple
past simple →past perfect
will →would
can →could

Questions: “Where do you live?” → She asked where I lived. (No question word order.)
Yes/no questions: “Are you ready?” → She asked if I was ready.
Commands: “Open the book.” → She told me to open the book.

Backshift is not always necessary, especially when a fact is still true or the reporting verb is present. Pronouns and time/place words change only when the context requires it.

Modal Verbs

Pattern: modal + base verb (without “to”), e.g. should study, not should to study.

MeaningCommon modalsExample
Abilitycan, couldShe can solve the problem.
Adviceshould, ought toYou should revise regularly.
Obligationmust, have toStudents must follow safety rules.
Prohibitionmustn'tYou mustn't copy answers.
No obligationdon't have toYou don't have to print it.
Possibilitymay, might, couldIt might rain tomorrow.
Deductionmust, can'tThe lights are on; she must be home.
Watch out: mustn't means something is prohibited; don't have to means it is not necessary. They are not the same.

Modal perfect: modal + have + past participle: “She might have forgotten.” / “You should have checked.”

Conditionals

TypeFormExample
Zero · general truthIf + present, presentIf you heat ice, it melts.
First · real future possibilityIf + present, will + baseIf she practises, she will improve.
Second · imagined present/futureIf + past, would + baseIf I had more time, I would read more.
Third · imagined pastIf + past perfect, would have + past participleIf she had revised, she would have passed.
Mixed · past condition, present resultIf + past perfect, would + baseIf I had slept earlier, I would feel better now.
The comma is normally used when the if-clause comes first. Other modal verbs (might, could) can sometimes replace will/would to change the meaning.

Relative Pronouns & Clauses — Complete Guide

A relative clause gives more information about a person, thing, place, time, or reason. The relative word connects the clause to the noun it describes.

WordUsed forExampleImportant point
whopeople (subject)The girl who won is in Grade 12.Who is the subject of won.
whompeople (object; formal)The teacher whom we thanked smiled.Who is common in everyday English.
whosepossessionThe student whose laptop broke called IT.Use for people and sometimes things.
whichthings and animalsThe project which won was creative.Can introduce non-defining clauses.
thatpeople or things in defining clausesThe book that I borrowed is useful.Do not use that after a comma in non-defining clauses.
whereplacesThis is the school where she studies.= in/at which in many contexts.
whentimesI remember the day when we met.= on which in many contexts.
whyreasonsThat is the reason why she left.Often omitted after reason.
Defining: The students who practised improved. (Essential information; no commas.)
Non-defining: Hessa, who practises daily, improved. (Extra information; use commas; not that.)
Omission rule: The book (that/which) I read was inspiring. The pronoun is an object, so it can be omitted. But: The student who won is here. Who is the subject, so it cannot be omitted.

Gerunds, To-Infinitives & Bare Infinitives

A gerund is verb + -ing used like a noun. A to-infinitive is to + base verb. A bare infinitive is the base verb without to. The pattern often depends on the first verb.

PatternCommon verbs / structuresCorrect example
verb + -ingenjoy, avoid, finish, consider, suggest, recommend, admit, deny, mind, practise, keep, miss, risk, postpone, imagine, give upShe enjoys reading. / They recommended visiting the museum.
verb + to + basewant, hope, plan, decide, agree, promise, refuse, offer, manage, afford, learn, expect, choose, fail, seem, pretendWe decided to revise.
verb + object + to + baseask, tell, advise, encourage, remind, persuade, invite, allow, teach, warn, expectThe teacher encouraged us to practise.
modal + bare infinitivecan, could, may, might, must, should, will, would, shallShe must finish her work.
let / make + object + bare infinitivelet, make (active voice)They let us leave. / The film made me cry.
help + object + (to) infinitivehelpShe helped me (to) understand.
had better / would rather + bare infinitivehad better, would ratherYou had better study. / I would rather stay.
preposition + -inginterested in, good at, look forward to, instead of, without, before, afterI look forward to meeting you.
Recommend and suggest: I recommend reading this book. / I recommend that she read it. / I recommend that she should read it. Do not write “I recommend her to read” in standard exam English. Likewise: I suggest going early / I suggest that we go early.

Verbs Followed by Both: Meaning Matters

Some verbs take either -ing or to-infinitive with little difference; others change meaning. Compare the pairs carefully.

Verb-ing example and meaningto-infinitive example and meaning
rememberI remember locking the door. (memory of past action)Remember to lock the door. (future duty)
forgetI will never forget meeting her. (past memory)Do not forget to email her. (future duty)
stopShe stopped talking. (ended activity)She stopped to talk. (paused another activity in order to talk)
tryTry restarting the laptop. (experiment with a method)Try to restart the laptop. (make an effort)
regretI regret saying that. (feel sorry about past action)We regret to inform you... (formal announcement)
meanBeing a leader means helping others. (involves)I meant to call you. (intended)
go onShe went on explaining. (continued same action)She went on to explain the results. (next action)
like / love / hate / preferI like reading. (general enjoyment)I like to read before bed. (habit/preference; often similar)
begin / start / continueIt started raining. (often same meaning)It started to rain. (often same meaning)
Passive exception: Active: The coach made the team run. Passive: The team was made to run. Also compare used to + base verb (past habit) and be used to + -ing (accustomed to something).

Verb Tenses & Forms

12 English Tenses

Rule: Choose time and aspect; compare the full twelve-form chart above.

Example: The students have completed the project.

Stative & Dynamic Verbs

Rule: Stative verbs usually describe states and do not normally take continuous forms.

Example: I understand the instructions.

Subject–Verb Agreement

Rule: In the present simple, he/she/it usually takes verb + s/es.

Example: The student studies; the students study.

Regular & Irregular Verbs

Rule: Regular verbs usually add -ed; irregular verbs have forms to memorise.

Example: They visited the museum and wrote a report.

Future Forms: Will / Going to / Present Continuous

Rule: Will can express decisions or predictions; going to can express plans or evidence-based predictions.

Example: The team is going to organise a reading event.

Used to / Be used to / Get used to

Rule: Used to + base: past habit; be/get used to + -ing: familiarity or adaptation.

Example: We are used to using digital tools.

Sentence Structure

Parts of Speech

Rule: Nouns name; verbs act; adjectives describe nouns; adverbs modify actions or qualities.

Example: The creative team worked carefully.

Sentence Types & Word Order

Rule: Statements typically follow subject–verb–object; questions often invert an auxiliary.

Example: The students designed a poster. Did they design it?

Simple, Compound & Complex Sentences

Rule: Simple: one independent clause; compound: two joined; complex: dependent + independent.

Example: We succeeded because we worked together.

Subject, Predicate, Objects & Complements

Rule: A subject is who/what the sentence is about; a predicate says something about it.

Example: The students elected Aisha leader.

Questions, Negatives & Question Tags

Rule: Use do/does/did + base in simple-tense questions; tags match auxiliaries.

Example: She volunteers, doesn’t she?

Voice & Reported Speech

Active & Passive Voice

Rule: Passive: appropriate form of be + V3; the focus shifts to the receiver.

Example: The class planted trees. → Trees were planted.

Direct & Reported Speech

Rule: Reported speech may change tense, pronouns and time words depending on context.

Example: “I am ready,” said Hessa. → Hessa said she was ready.

Reported Questions, Commands & Requests

Rule: Reported questions use statement word order; commands often use ask/tell + object + to.

Example: She asked where the exhibition was.

Causative Have / Get

Rule: Have/get + object + V3 describes arranging a service.

Example: We had the exhibition posters printed.

Modal Verbs & Conditionals

Modal Verbs and Their Functions

Rule: Modal + base verb expresses advice, possibility, obligation, ability or permission.

Example: We should conserve water.

Modal Perfects

Rule: Modal + have + V3 expresses past possibility, deduction or criticism.

Example: They might have missed the announcement.

Zero, First, Second & Third Conditionals

Rule: Zero: general; first: possible future; second: hypothetical; third: unreal past.

Example: If we plan carefully, we will succeed.

Mixed Conditionals

Rule: Combine an unreal past condition with a present result (or vice versa).

Example: If we had prepared earlier, we would be ready now.

Wish / If Only

Rule: Wish + past: unreal present; wish + past perfect: past regret.

Example: I wish I knew more about solar energy.

Clauses & Linking Words

Coordinating Conjunctions

Rule: For, and, nor, but, or, yet, so join equal grammatical elements.

Example: We researched the topic, and we shared our findings.

Subordinating Conjunctions

Rule: Because, although, if, when, while, unless, since introduce dependent clauses.

Example: Although the task was difficult, the team succeeded.

Correlative Conjunctions

Rule: Both…and; either…or; neither…nor; not only…but also connect parallel elements.

Example: Both the science club and the art club participated.

Relative Clauses: Defining & Non-defining

Rule: Defining clauses identify a noun; non-defining clauses add comma-separated information.

Example: The student who led the campaign received a certificate.

Adverbial Clauses

Rule: Dependent clauses explain time, reason, purpose, condition or contrast.

Example: We checked our sources before we presented.

Linking Words & Transition Signals

Rule: However signals contrast; therefore signals result; furthermore adds information.

Example: The idea was ambitious. However, it was achievable.

Nouns, Pronouns & Determiners

Countable & Uncountable Nouns

Rule: Countables take plural forms; uncountables generally do not.

Example: We collected useful information and three photographs.

Articles: A / An / The

Rule: A/an: non-specific singular countable; the: identifiable; zero article for many general nouns.

Example: A student suggested an idea. The idea worked.

Quantifiers

Rule: Many/few with countables; much/little with uncountables; some with both.

Example: Many students shared a little information.

Pronouns & Possessives

Rule: Subject, object, possessive adjective and possessive pronoun have distinct roles.

Example: Hessa brought her notebook. It is hers.

Determiners

Rule: This/that/these/those, each/every and some/any specify nouns.

Example: Each student wrote a reflection.

Adjectives, Adverbs & Prepositions

Comparatives & Superlatives

Rule: Use -er/-est or more/most; memorise irregular forms such as good–better–best.

Example: This solution is better and more practical.

Adjective Order

Rule: Adjectives commonly follow opinion–size–age–shape–colour–origin–material–purpose.

Example: The group displayed a beautiful traditional Emirati craft.

Adverbs & Their Position

Rule: Adverbs show manner, frequency, time or degree; placement depends on type.

Example: She always checks her work carefully.

Prepositions of Time, Place & Movement

Rule: At for clock times, on for days, in for months/years; to/into/across for movement.

Example: The workshop starts at nine on Monday in October.

Adjective + Preposition / Verb + Preposition

Rule: Learn fixed combinations in context.

Example: The students are proud of their achievements.

Advanced Grammar

Gerunds & Infinitives

Rule: Some verbs take -ing; others take to + base; some take both with changed meaning.

Example: The students enjoy reading and hope to publish.

Participles & Participle Clauses

Rule: -ing often expresses active meaning; V3 often expresses passive meaning.

Example: Inspired by the workshop, the students wrote stories.

Inversion

Rule: After some negative/limiting expressions, place the auxiliary before the subject.

Example: Never have I seen such a creative exhibition.

Emphasis & Cleft Sentences

Rule: It-clefts and what-clefts highlight a chosen sentence element.

Example: It was the students who organised the campaign.

Parallel Structure

Rule: Items in a series should follow the same grammatical form.

Example: The project involved researching, designing and presenting.

Punctuation in Complex Sentences

Rule: Use commas after introductory dependent clauses; non-defining relative clauses need paired commas.

Example: Although it was raining, the students arrived on time.

Reduced Relative Clauses

Rule: Some relative clauses can shorten to participle phrases without changing meaning.

Example: Students participating in the event received certificates.

Irregular Verbs · V1 / V2 / V3

Use V1 (base form) after modal verbs and in many present forms; V2 (past simple) for completed past events; and V3 (past participle) with perfect tenses and passive voice.

V1: We write reports.
V2: We wrote a report yesterday.
V3: We have written three reports.

Patterns: sing–sang–sung · buy–bought–bought · cut–cut–cut. Some forms have accepted regional alternatives. “Read” is spelled the same in all three forms but its past pronunciation changes.
