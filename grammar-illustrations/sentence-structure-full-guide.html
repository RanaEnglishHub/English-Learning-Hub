1. Parts of Speech

Learn to recognise what each word does in a sentence.

ConceptExplanationIllustrated exampleRemember
NounNames a person, place, thing or ideaThe students showed creativity.Common, proper, abstract, collective and uncountable nouns
PronounReplaces or refers to a nounThey presented their work.Personal, possessive, reflexive, relative and indefinite pronouns
VerbShows action or stateShe has prepared a presentation.Main verbs, auxiliary verbs and modal verbs
AdjectiveDescribes a noun or pronounIt was an innovative project.Usually before nouns or after linking verbs
AdverbModifies a verb, adjective, adverb or whole clauseShe explained the idea clearly.Manner, frequency, time, place and degree
PrepositionShows relationships of time, place or other meaningsThe meeting starts at eight.at/on/in for time; in/on/under for place
ConjunctionJoins words, phrases or clausesWe continued although it was late.Coordinating, subordinating and correlative
InterjectionExpresses a reactionWow! That is impressive.Usually separated by punctuation
Compare and correct: Compare: hard (adjective/adverb) vs hardly (almost not); good (adjective) vs well (usually adverb).

2. Sentence Types & Word Order

Understand sentence purpose and arrange words correctly.

ConceptExplanationIllustrated exampleRemember
DeclarativeMakes a statementThe students completed the survey.Ends with a full stop
InterrogativeAsks a questionDid the students complete the survey?Auxiliary often comes before subject
ImperativeGives an instruction or requestComplete the survey, please.The subject you is usually understood
ExclamatoryExpresses strong feelingWhat an inspiring project!Ends with an exclamation mark
Statement orderSubject + verb + object/complementHessa designed a poster.Subject before the main verb
Yes/no questionAuxiliary + subject + main verbHas Hessa finished?Use do/does/did if needed
Wh-questionQuestion word + auxiliary + subject + verbWhy did they leave?Subject questions: Who called? (no do)
Adverb placementFrequency adverbs usually precede main verbsShe often studies; she is always prepared.After be, before most main verbs
Compare and correct: Incorrect: Why she left? → Correct: Why did she leave? But: Do you know why she left? (embedded question).

3. Simple, Compound & Complex Sentences

Build longer, clearer sentences with clauses.

ConceptExplanationIllustrated exampleRemember
Independent clauseHas a subject and finite verb; stands aloneThe team practised.Can form a simple sentence
Dependent clauseHas a subject and verb but cannot stand aloneBecause the team practisedNeeds an independent clause
Simple sentenceOne independent clauseThe students researched the topic.May have compound subjects or verbs
Compound sentenceTwo independent clauses joined correctlyWe researched, and we presented.Comma + FANBOYS; or semicolon
Complex sentenceIndependent + dependent clauseAlthough it rained, we continued.Comma usually follows an initial dependent clause
Compound-complexAt least two independent + one dependent clauseAlthough it rained, we continued, and we finished.Combines both patterns
Relative clauseAdds information about a nounThe student who won smiled.Defining vs non-defining
Sentence fragmentIncomplete dependent clauseBecause she was late. → She hurried because she was late.Do not leave a dependent clause alone
Compare and correct: Comma splice: She revised, she passed. → She revised, and she passed. / She revised; she passed.

4. Subjects, Predicates, Objects & Complements

Label the functions of phrases and words.

ConceptExplanationIllustrated exampleRemember
SubjectWho/what the clause is aboutThe young scientist discovered a solution.A noun phrase or clause
PredicateSays something about the subjectThe scientist discovered a solution.Includes verb and its complements/modifiers
Direct objectReceives the action of a transitive verbShe wrote a report.Ask wrote what?
Indirect objectReceives or benefits from somethingShe gave her friend a report.Can often use to/for: gave a report to her friend
Subject complementDescribes or renames subject after linking verbThe experiment was successful.Adjective or noun phrase
Object complementDescribes or renames the objectThey elected Aisha leader.The object becomes/is called the complement
AdverbialGives information such as when, where, howThey met after school.May be optional or required
Transitive vs intransitiveSome verbs take objects; others do notShe opened the door; the door opened.Not every verb can be made passive
Compare and correct: Compare: She became a leader (subject complement) vs They made her leader (object complement).

5. Questions, Negatives & Question Tags

Make accurate questions, negative sentences and tags.

ConceptExplanationIllustrated exampleRemember
Present simple questionDo/does + subject + base verbDoes she study English?Not: Does she studies?
Past simple questionDid + subject + base verbDid they finish?Not: Did they finished?
Negative present/pastdo/does/did + not + base verbShe does not agree.Contractions: don’t, doesn’t, didn’t
Be/have/modalsInvert existing auxiliaryIs she ready? Can she join?Do not add do to be or modals
Positive statement → negative tagMatch tense, auxiliary and pronounShe is ready, isn’t she?Positive → negative
Negative statement → positive tagMatch tense, auxiliary and pronounThey didn’t leave, did they?Negative → positive
Special tag: I amCommon form: aren’t I?I’m next, aren’t I?Special conventional pattern
Imperative and let’swill you? / shall we?Open the window, will you? Let’s begin, shall we?Meaning and tone matter
Compare and correct: Tricky tags: Everyone is ready, aren’t they? and She never complains, does she? (“never” makes the statement negative).

6. Causative Have & Get

Explain arranging for a service or causing someone to act.

ConceptExplanationIllustrated exampleRemember
Have + object + V3Arrange for something to be doneShe had her laptop repaired.The subject usually does not perform the repair
Get + object + V3Arrange for something to be doneShe got her laptop repaired.Often more conversational
Have + person + base verbAsk/instruct someone to do somethingThe teacher had the students revise.Not: had the students to revise
Get + person + to-infinitivePersuade/arrange for someone to do somethingShe got her friend to help.Get + person + to + verb
Causative vs ordinary pastDifferent agentsShe cut her hair / She had her hair cut.The second usually implies another person did it
Tense changesChange have/get, not V3She will have the room painted.V3 stays the same
Negative causativeNegate have/get with auxiliariesShe didn’t get the phone fixed.Didn’t + get
Unpleasant experienceHave/get + object + V3 may describe something that happenedHe had his bag stolen.Not always arranged deliberately
Compare and correct: Contrast: I had the technician check the printer vs I got the technician to check the printer.
