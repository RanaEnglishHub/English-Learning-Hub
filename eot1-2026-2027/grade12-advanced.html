Grammar Quick Guide — Understand the Rule Before the MAZE

These are brief student-friendly reminders for the grammar topics in your 2026–2027 EOT 1 planner. Study the examples, then practise all four Grammar MAZE passages. For detailed illustrated lessons, open the full reference below.

1. Zero conditional

Use if/when + present simple, present simple for facts and regular results.

Example: If rainwater collects, plants grow.

2. Comparative adjectives

Use -er or more + adjective with than to compare two people, places or things; use better and worse for irregular forms.

Example: Tokyo is more densely populated than many cities.

3. Adverbs of quantity

Use much with uncountable nouns, many with countable plurals, and a lot of with both. Use few/a few and little/a little carefully.

Example: Many buildings use technology, but little energy is wasted.

4. Present simple passive

Use am/is/are + past participle (V3) when describing general facts and processes.

Example: Solar panels are installed on rooftops.

5. Indirect questions

After Could you explain…? or Do you know…?, use statement word order.

Example: Do you know how smart homes save electricity?

6. Adverbial linkers

Use however for contrast, therefore for result, and furthermore for addition; punctuate carefully.

Example: The system is expensive; however, it saves energy.

7. Sequencing adverbs

Use first, next, then, afterwards and finally to show the order of events or stages.

Example: First, sensors collect data. Next, the system adjusts the lighting.

1. Personality: Born or Shaped? 3A · At grade level · 356 words · 8 questions

Personality: Born or Shaped?

Why do two people respond differently to the same challenge? Some researchers investigate inherited tendencies, while others focus on family life, education and experience. The debate about nature and nurture is not simply a choice between two competing explanations.

Consider two students who join a public-speaking club. One appears comfortable immediately, while the other feels anxious before every presentation. Their different reactions may reflect temperament, previous experiences or both. After several weeks of practice, the anxious student becomes more confident.

This improvement does not prove that inherited traits are unimportant. Instead, it illustrates how experiences can influence the expression of a tendency. Researchers also warn that a single observation cannot establish the cause of a complex behaviour.

The question matters in education because labels can affect expectations. If a student is described as naturally shy, adults may offer fewer speaking opportunities. A more useful approach recognises individual differences while providing chances to develop new skills.

The examples also show why evidence must be interpreted carefully. One experience can illustrate a wider issue, but it cannot prove that everyone will respond in the same way. Comparing several viewpoints makes the final judgement more reliable.

For students, the practical lesson is to ask what a claim means, who benefits from it and what information is missing. A careful reader separates an observed fact from a prediction or a personal preference.

2. Megacities and Smaller Communities 3B · Beyond grade level · 359 words · 8 questions

Megacities and Smaller Communities

Growing cities attract people because they offer jobs, transport links and specialised services. Yet rapid urbanisation also creates difficult questions about housing, congestion, green space and access to public facilities. Comparing large and small communities requires more than counting residents.

A megacity may provide extensive public transport and universities, but long journeys can still reduce residents' free time. A smaller town may offer quieter streets and stronger local connections, while having fewer specialised hospitals or career opportunities. Neither setting automatically meets every person's needs.

Urban planners increasingly examine how infrastructure affects daily life. Well-designed drainage can reduce flooding, and connected green areas can support biodiversity. These improvements require funding and maintenance, not simply attractive designs on a planning document.

Residents also disagree about priorities. Some value convenient services and cultural events; others prefer affordable housing and open space. A thoughtful comparison must acknowledge these different perspectives rather than assuming that one lifestyle suits everyone.

3. The Personality Debate in a Twin Study 3A · At grade level · 341 words · 8 questions

The Personality Debate in a Twin Study

Identical twins are sometimes described as a natural experiment because they share nearly all their inherited genetic material. Yet this description can be misleading if it suggests that their lives unfold in identical ways. Consider two twins who grew up together but later entered different professions. One became an emergency nurse; the other chose landscape architecture. Both were calm as children, but their adult responses to pressure differed considerably.

A researcher might interpret their childhood similarities as evidence that inherited traits influence temperament. However, the differences that emerged later could reflect training, colleagues, responsibilities and repeated experiences. The nurse learned to make rapid decisions in urgent situations, while the architect developed patience for projects that took years to complete. Neither explanation is sufficient on its own.

Some commentators argue that genetic influence places firm limits on personal change. They point to studies showing that certain characteristics remain relatively stable across time. Others counter that stability is not the same as inevitability: a tendency towards caution, for instance, may be expressed differently in a supportive environment than in a threatening one.

There is also a methodological challenge. Researchers cannot always separate inherited characteristics from the environments families create. Parents may pass on both genes and habits, and children may seek experiences that suit their existing preferences. These interactions make simple percentages difficult to interpret.

The most defensible conclusion is not that biology and experience contribute equally to every trait. Rather, personality develops through relationships between predispositions and circumstances, with the balance varying across individuals and stages of life. The twins' contrasting careers illustrate this complexity without proving a universal rule. A responsible account of human behaviour must therefore consider both patterns in research and the limits of individual examples.

To investigate such questions, scientists may follow participants for many years rather than relying on a single interview. They can compare observations from relatives, teachers and colleagues, while acknowledging that each method introduces possible bias. Long-term evidence strengthens an argument, but it does not turn a complex relationship into a simple cause-and-effect formula.

4. Dubai and Tokyo: Different Answers to Urban Pressure 3B · Above grade level · 333 words · 8 questions

Dubai and Tokyo: Different Answers to Urban Pressure

Tokyo and Dubai are frequently presented as examples of ambitious urban development, yet the challenges they face are not identical. Tokyo has a long-established metropolitan rail network and an exceptionally dense built environment. Dubai has expanded rapidly across a hotter, more spacious landscape, investing in roads, rail links and landmark districts. Comparing them requires more than ranking their skylines.

Transport illustrates the contrast. Tokyo's extensive railway connections allow many residents to move without driving, although crowded stations can make peak-hour travel exhausting. Dubai's Metro provides an important alternative to cars, but some neighbourhoods remain more convenient to reach by road. Urban planners in both cities must consider not only speed but also accessibility, cost and the needs of different residents.

Climate creates another distinction. Tokyo experiences humid summers and seasonal changes, while Dubai faces prolonged periods of intense heat. Buildings in both cities need effective insulation and energy management, yet Dubai's conditions make shade and cooling particularly urgent. Green roofs and vegetation can moderate local temperatures, but their benefits depend on maintenance and water use.

Supporters of megacities emphasise specialised employment, cultural opportunities and efficient shared infrastructure. Critics point to congestion, high housing costs and the pressure of living in crowded districts. Neither argument is complete without considering whose experience is being described: a commuter, a family and a visitor may judge the same city differently.

The comparison therefore raises a more useful question than which city is “better.” How can each adapt its strengths to future demands while reducing environmental costs and improving everyday life? Tokyo's transport experience and Dubai's experimentation with new urban systems offer lessons, but no solution can be copied without attention to local conditions.

Housing is another useful point of comparison. Access to employment does not guarantee that workers can afford to live near their workplaces. Long commutes may increase stress even when transport is reliable. Policies that support mixed neighbourhoods, public spaces and affordable services can therefore influence quality of life as much as iconic buildings do.

2. Which advantage is associated with Tokyo?

4. Why is shade especially important in Dubai?
5. What does “moderate” mean in relation to temperatures?
6. What can be inferred about copying urban solutions?
7. Which perspective is missing from a simple skyline ranking?

Topic 1: How does daily life in a megacity differ from life in a small town?

Three guiding questions
  1. What opportunities do megacities offer for education, employment and transport?
  2. How do smaller towns differ in cost of living, community life and the environment?
  3. Would you prefer to live in a megacity or a small town, and which differences influence your choice?
Study the full annotated model essay

Introduction

[Hook] A city can offer remarkable opportunities, but a good life depends on more than its skyline. [Background information] Megacities provide extensive transport networks, universities and workplaces, while smaller towns often offer quieter surroundings and closer communities. [Thesis statement] Although both settings can support a satisfying life, I would choose a smaller town because of its calmer environment and stronger sense of community.

Body Paragraph 1

[Topic sentence] Megacities attract residents through their opportunities and services. [Supporting detail] Large cities often connect people with specialised jobs, hospitals and cultural activities. [Example / evidence] For example, an extensive public transport network can make it easier to reach different workplaces. For example, a household may save electricity through simple insulation and careful ventilation without relying on an expensive network of connected devices. At the same time, well-designed automation can reduce waste when residents understand its settings and protect their personal data.

Body Paragraph 2

[Topic sentence] Smaller towns, however, may offer a more relaxed daily routine. [Supporting detail] Shorter journeys and less congestion can give residents more time for family and outdoor activities. [Example / evidence] For instance, children may be able to walk to local parks without spending a long time in traffic.

Body Paragraph 3

[Topic sentence] Nevertheless, smaller towns can have fewer specialist services. [Supporting detail] Residents may need to travel to a large city for certain medical treatments or university courses. [Example / evidence] This disadvantage matters, but it can be reduced by improved regional transport and online services.

Conclusion

[Restated thesis] In conclusion, megacities and small towns offer different strengths and limitations. [Final thought] I prefer the quieter pace and community connections of a small town, although city life may be better for someone seeking particular career opportunities.

Topic 2: Tokyo and Dubai: Comparing Two Megacities

Three guiding questions
  1. How do Tokyo and Dubai differ in their transport systems and urban design?
  2. What similarities do they share in technology, services and opportunities?
  3. Which city would you prefer to live in, and what makes it more suitable for you?
Study the full annotated model essay

Introduction

[Hook] A city can offer millions of opportunities, yet the experience of living there depends on much more than its size. [Background information] Tokyo in Japan and Dubai in the United Arab Emirates are internationally connected urban centres known for ambitious development. Both attract people seeking employment, education and new experiences, but their transport systems, climates and urban lifestyles differ. [Thesis statement] Although Tokyo and Dubai both offer advanced infrastructure and exciting opportunities, I would prefer Dubai for its modern planning, international environment and familiarity with life in the UAE.

Body Paragraph 1

[Topic sentence] Both cities invest in infrastructure, but they approach daily mobility in different ways. [Supporting detail] Tokyo is especially well known for its extensive rail network, which connects many neighbourhoods and makes public transport central to everyday life. [Example / evidence] For example, commuters can use trains and subways to reach work, shops and schools across the metropolitan area. [Supporting detail] Dubai also has a metro system and major road networks, although travel patterns often involve a combination of public transport, taxis and private vehicles.

Body Paragraph 2

[Topic sentence] Climate and urban lifestyle create another important contrast between the two cities. [Supporting detail] Tokyo experiences four distinct seasons, whereas Dubai has a hot desert climate with especially warm summers. [Example / evidence] For instance, Tokyo residents may enjoy seasonal changes such as spring blossoms, while Dubai residents often choose indoor activities during the hottest months. [Supporting detail] Despite these differences, both cities provide cultural attractions, shopping areas and spaces where people from varied backgrounds can connect.

Body Paragraph 3

[Topic sentence] In terms of future development, both cities face sustainability challenges and are exploring ways to improve urban living. [Supporting detail] Dense populations require careful management of energy, transport, buildings and public spaces. [Example / evidence] Tokyo can build on its established public-transport culture, while Dubai continues to develop cleaner mobility options and energy-efficient buildings. [Supporting detail] For me, Dubai has an additional advantage because its multicultural setting and proximity to family would make settling there easier.

Smart Homes: Convenience and Caution — Summary practice 1 195 words

Supporters of smart homes argue that connected devices make daily routines more convenient and efficient. Automated lighting, temperature controls and security alerts can help residents manage their homes remotely. Some systems also provide information about electricity use, encouraging households to reduce unnecessary consumption. For older residents or people with mobility difficulties, voice controls may increase independence. Critics, however, question the reliability and privacy of connected systems. Devices that collect household data can create risks if security settings are weak, and technical failures may interrupt essential functions. Upfront installation costs can also make advanced systems inaccessible to some families. Both viewpoints recognise that technology should serve residents rather than complicate their lives. Supporters emphasise personalised convenience and potential energy savings, whereas critics prioritise affordability, data protection and resilience. A practical compromise is to introduce selected devices with clear privacy controls and manual alternatives. The value of a smart home therefore depends on its design, maintenance and the needs of its occupants. Comparing both positions suggests that innovation is most beneficial when residents understand the trade-offs and retain meaningful control over their living environment. A balanced evaluation should distinguish short-term convenience from lasting social and environmental effects.

Megacities and Small Towns — Summary practice 2 207 words

Advocates of megacity living point to specialised employment, universities, hospitals and extensive transport networks. They argue that large populations can support cultural institutions and public services that smaller communities cannot easily provide. A young professional may find more career options in a major city, while students may benefit from access to different courses. Supporters of small towns emphasise quieter streets, shorter journeys and closer relationships between neighbours. They suggest that lower congestion and easier access to nature can improve everyday well-being. Critics of megacities highlight housing costs, pollution and long commutes, although they acknowledge that good planning can reduce these problems. Critics of small towns note that specialised services and jobs may be limited, requiring residents to travel. Both perspectives define quality of life differently. The first gives greater weight to opportunity and variety; the second values space, community and a manageable pace. Neither setting is automatically superior for every household. Transport links, housing policies and digital services can also narrow the differences between them. A fair comparison must consider personal priorities as well as environmental and economic conditions. A balanced evaluation should distinguish short-term convenience from lasting social and environmental effects. The needs of different households and communities must also be considered before drawing broad conclusions.

Online and Classroom Learning — Summary practice 3 209 words

Supporters of online learning emphasise flexibility and access. Students can review recorded explanations, organise study around other responsibilities and join courses that are unavailable nearby. Digital platforms may also offer instant feedback and varied learning resources. Critics argue that remote lessons can reduce spontaneous discussion and make it harder for some learners to remain motivated. Unreliable internet connections or limited access to devices may widen existing inequalities. Classroom-learning advocates value direct interaction with teachers and classmates, especially during experiments, debates and collaborative projects. They also point to routines that encourage attendance and concentration. However, traditional classrooms can be less flexible for students who need different pacing or live far from educational centres. Both sides agree that successful learning requires clear goals, meaningful feedback and opportunities to practise. Their disagreement concerns which environment provides those conditions most consistently. Blended approaches may combine recorded resources with face-to-face discussion and supervised activities. Rather than assuming one format is universally better, schools should consider subject requirements, student needs and access to technology. Educational quality depends on thoughtful teaching as much as on the delivery method. A balanced evaluation should distinguish short-term convenience from lasting social and environmental effects.
