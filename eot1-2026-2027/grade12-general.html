Grammar Quick Guide — Understand the Rule Before the MAZE

These are brief student-friendly reminders for the grammar topics in your 2026–2027 EOT 1 planner. Study the examples, then practise all four Grammar MAZE passages. For detailed illustrated lessons, open the full reference below.

1. Verb patterns: to-infinitive, -ing and base form

Use to + verb after want, decide and hope; use verb + -ing after enjoy, avoid and suggest. After modals, use the base form. Some verbs, such as stop and remember, change meaning with -ing versus to + verb.

Example: She hopes to study engineering. / He enjoys solving problems. / Students must arrive on time.

2. Present continuous passive

Use am/is/are + being + past participle (V3) for an action happening now when the receiver is the subject.

Example: New skills are being taught this term.

3. Non-defining relative clauses

Add extra information using who, which or whose between commas. Do not use that in non-defining clauses.

Example: My aunt, who works in healthcare, enjoys her job.

4. Reported speech

Report someone’s words using said or told. When the reporting verb is in the past, present forms often move back one tense; pronouns and time words may change.

Example: “I am working,” she said. → She said that she was working.

5. Adverbs of degree

Use very, extremely, quite, too and enough to show intensity. Too suggests more than acceptable; enough means sufficient.

Example: The interview was extremely challenging. / She is confident enough to apply.

6. Subordinating conjunctions

Use because, although, if, when, while and unless to join a dependent clause to a main clause. Add a comma when the dependent clause comes first.

Example: Although the job was demanding, she enjoyed it.

7. Present simple passive

Use am/is/are + past participle (V3) for general processes, routines and facts when the action matters more than the doer.

Example: Applications are reviewed every week.

1. Why We Buy More Than We Need 3A · At grade level · 334 words · 8 questions

Why We Buy More Than We Need

A purchase may begin with a practical need, but the final decision is often influenced by feelings. Advertisements use colour, repetition and social approval to make products appear desirable. Understanding these methods can help consumers make choices they will not regret.

A student research group compared two online advertisements for the same reusable bottle. One focused on price and durability; the other showed fashionable images and a message about belonging. Students who saw the second advertisement were more likely to describe the bottle as exciting, even though both advertisements described an identical product.

The result did not prove that advertising controls everyone. Some students compared prices, read reviews and considered whether they already owned a suitable bottle. Others admitted that a limited-time offer made them feel they had to decide quickly.

Financial educators recommend a simple pause before buying: identify the need, compare alternatives and check the budget. This does not mean refusing every enjoyable purchase. It means recognising the difference between a planned choice and a reaction to pressure.

The examples also show why evidence must be interpreted carefully. One experience can illustrate a wider issue, but it cannot prove that everyone will respond in the same way. Comparing several viewpoints makes the final judgement more reliable.

For students, the practical lesson is to ask what a claim means, who benefits from it and what information is missing. A careful reader separates an observed fact from a prediction or a personal preference.

Small choices can produce important long-term results when they are repeated consistently. However, solutions work best when they reflect the needs and circumstances of the people involved.

Changing Ideas About Future Careers

Ideas about suitable careers have changed as workplaces adopt new technology and flexible schedules. Many jobs now require communication, adaptability and a willingness to learn throughout life. Traditional assumptions about who should perform particular roles are increasingly questioned.

A careers fair invited engineers, nurses, designers and entrepreneurs to speak with students. Instead of describing their work as a list of tasks, the visitors explained the problems they solve. One engineer described improving public transport; a nurse discussed making difficult decisions with patients and families.

Students noticed that the most useful skills appeared across several occupations. Teamwork helped people share information, while critical thinking helped them evaluate unfamiliar situations. Technical knowledge remained important, but speakers agreed that it needed regular updating.

Some students worried that changing career expectations would make choosing a profession harder. The visitors argued that uncertainty could also create opportunities. Exploring internships and asking informed questions can reveal interests that a job title alone does not show.

3. The Hidden Cost of Easy Payments 3A · At grade level · 299 words · 8 questions

The Hidden Cost of Easy Payments

A university student named Salma noticed that many online shops offered a choice: pay the full price immediately or divide it into several smaller payments. The second option seemed harmless, particularly when the advertisement described it as interest-free. Salma wondered whether these offers changed what customers decided to buy.

For a class project, she asked thirty volunteers to compare two versions of the same shopping page. One displayed the total price prominently; the other emphasised the smaller instalment. Participants using the instalment page were more likely to add an unnecessary item to their baskets. The activity was limited in size, so Salma did not claim that it proved how all shoppers behaved.

She also interviewed a financial adviser, who explained that spreading payments could be useful when planned carefully. The risk came from making several purchases without considering their combined cost. A person might manage each instalment separately but struggle to make ends meet when multiple payments became due together.

Salma then revised her own budget. She decided to put aside part of her income before spending on entertainment and to check the total cost of every purchase, not only the amount due that week. Her conclusion was not that instalments were always harmful. Instead, she argued that the way a price is presented can influence decisions, especially when shoppers are tired or in a hurry.

The project reminded her classmates that financial independence involves more than earning money. It also requires recognising persuasive techniques and thinking about future obligations before accepting an apparently convenient offer.

Another volunteer observed that the smaller figure drew attention away from the overall commitment. Salma included this comment in her report but treated it as one perspective, not conclusive evidence. She recommended comparing offers with a written monthly budget before making a decision.

2. What happened on the instalment page?
3. Why did Salma avoid broad conclusions?
4. What does “make ends meet” mean?
5. When might instalments be helpful?
6. What can be inferred about small weekly payments?

4. Who Belongs in a Technology Career? 3B · Above grade level · 309 words · 8 questions

Who Belongs in a Technology Career?

When a technology company announced a school internship, several students assumed that the successful applicants would be those who could write computer code fastest. Yet the recruitment team had a broader set of requirements. Applicants would help design an app for older residents, so the company wanted people who could listen, communicate clearly and adapt when an idea failed.

The first shortlisted candidate, Noor, had built a simple website. She also volunteered at a community centre, where she had learned to explain unfamiliar devices patiently. Another candidate, Dana, had little programming experience but had organised a school exhibition involving several departments. Her application described how she handled disagreements and adjusted plans when equipment arrived late.

During the interviews, the team gave candidates a practical challenge. They had to explain a complicated feature to someone with no technical background. Some confident programmers used specialist vocabulary that confused the listener. Others asked questions first and changed their explanations in response. The interviewers valued that flexibility.

One manager noted that technical skills remained essential. However, she argued that modern occupations rarely involve working alone on a single task. Designers, researchers and customer-support staff often cooperate, and their work changes as new tools become available. An internship should therefore offer opportunities to develop both specialised knowledge and transferable skills.

The company eventually selected a mixed group rather than candidates with identical backgrounds. The decision did not mean that qualifications were irrelevant. It showed that experience can be gained in different settings, and that assumptions about the “ideal” technology worker may prevent employers from recognising talent.

After the selection, the team arranged mentoring sessions for every intern. Experienced staff would teach technical tools, while the interns would practise presenting ideas to different audiences. This approach recognised that an internship is a learning opportunity, not merely a competition between applicants who already possess every skill.

1. What was the internship team designing?
2. What strengthened Noor’s application?
3. Why is Dana’s exhibition experience relevant?
4. What did the interview task measure?
5. What does “transferable skills” suggest?
6. Which statement best reflects the manager’s view?
7. What can be inferred about confident programmers?
8. What assumption does the text challenge?

Body Paragraph 1

[Topic sentence] One important consideration is the effect on individual development. [Supporting detail] When people think carefully about should teenagers learn to manage a budget, they learn to evaluate choices rather than follow habits or pressure. [Example / evidence] For example, a student who compares alternatives, asks questions and plans ahead is more likely to recognise both opportunities and possible difficulties. [Supporting detail] This ability becomes useful beyond the immediate situation.

Body Paragraph 2

[Topic sentence] A second consideration is the wider impact on other people and the community. [Supporting detail] Good decisions depend on communication, fairness and an understanding that people do not all share the same needs. [Example / evidence] For instance, a school project may succeed when students listen to different opinions and adjust their plan to include everyone. [Supporting detail] Such experiences show why responsible action matters as much as a convincing argument.

Body Paragraph 3

[Topic sentence] Some people argue that changing existing practices is unnecessary or too demanding. [Supporting detail] They may point to limited time, financial pressures or uncertainty about the results. [Example / evidence] However, these concerns can often be addressed through realistic planning, gradual improvement and support from families or schools. [Supporting detail] The possibility of difficulty is therefore not a sufficient reason to reject a thoughtful approach.

Conclusion

[Restated thesis] In conclusion, should teenagers learn to manage a budget should be judged through careful reasoning rather than assumptions. [Summary] The strongest decisions consider personal development, effects on others and the practical challenges involved. [Final thought] By combining evidence with responsibility, young people can make choices that benefit both themselves and their communities.

Teenagers and Budgeting — Summary practice 1 187 words

Teenagers make financial choices whenever they spend pocket money, purchase online services or save for something important. A budget helps them see how much money is available and decide which expenses matter most. For example, someone saving for a laptop might reduce spending on snacks or entertainment. Keeping a simple record of purchases can also reveal small costs that add up over several weeks. Some teenagers dislike budgets because they believe planning removes freedom. Others argue that knowing where money goes actually makes independent choices easier. A useful budget should be realistic rather than overly strict. It can include necessary expenses, a savings goal and a small amount for enjoyment. Unexpected costs should also be considered. Schools can introduce budgeting through practical examples without asking students to share private family finances. Learning to compare prices and recognise misleading offers is equally important. Budgeting cannot solve every money problem, especially when income is limited, but it encourages thoughtful decisions. Over time, students may develop habits that help them manage larger responsibilities after graduation. Discussing realistic examples helps learners connect classroom knowledge with decisions they may face outside school.

University and Alternative Pathways — Summary practice 2 175 words

Many students see university as a route to professional knowledge, recognised qualifications and new opportunities. Degree programmes may be essential for careers that require specialised academic training. University can also develop research, communication and independent-learning skills. However, it is not the only path to a successful career. Vocational programmes teach practical skills for industries that need trained technicians and specialists. Apprenticeships allow learners to gain experience while working with experienced employees. Some young people also develop businesses or pursue further training after beginning employment. Choosing a pathway requires more than comparing prestige. Students should investigate entry requirements, costs, interests and future job opportunities. They may also benefit from speaking with career advisers and people already working in the field. Supporters of university education emphasise broad academic development, while supporters of vocational training value direct workplace preparation. Both groups agree that learning should continue after graduation. A strong career plan can combine formal qualifications with experience and new skills. The best route depends on the person and the occupation rather than a single rule for everyone.

Responsible Advertising — Summary practice 3 187 words

Online advertisements often appear beside entertainment and social media posts, making them difficult to ignore. Businesses use these messages to introduce products, explain services and reach potential customers. When claims are accurate, advertising can help people compare prices and make informed purchases. Problems arise when promotions exaggerate results or hide important conditions. Teenagers may be especially influenced by celebrities, limited-time offers and carefully edited images. Some people favour stricter rules for advertisements aimed at young audiences. They argue that companies should clearly identify sponsored content and avoid misleading promises. Others believe education is more effective than broad restrictions because young people need to learn how to evaluate messages independently. Both views recognise that consumers require trustworthy information. Schools can teach students to examine evidence, compare sources and question emotional language. Families can also discuss how advertising affects spending habits. Responsible marketing and critical thinking are not competing goals: they can work together to protect customers. Before purchasing, teenagers should consider whether an item is necessary and whether the offer matches their budget.
