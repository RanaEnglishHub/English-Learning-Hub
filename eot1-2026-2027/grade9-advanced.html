Grammar Quick Guide — Understand the Rule Before the MAZE

These are brief student-friendly reminders for the grammar topics in your 2026–2027 EOT 1 planner. Study the examples, then practise all four Grammar MAZE passages. For detailed illustrated lessons, open the full reference below.

1. Past continuous

Use was/were + verb-ing for an action in progress at a past time or interrupted by another event.

Example: The students were studying when the storm began.

2. Defining relative clauses

Use who for people, which for things, that for people or things, and whose for possession. These clauses identify exactly who or what we mean; do not use commas.

Example: The student who shares my studio is organised.

3. Verb patterns: to-infinitive, -ing and base form

Use to + verb after want, decide and hope; use verb + -ing after enjoy, avoid and suggest. After modals, use the base form. Some verbs, such as stop and remember, change meaning with -ing versus to + verb.

Example: She hopes to study engineering. / He enjoys solving problems. / Students must arrive on time.

4. Indirect questions

Use introductory phrases such as Could you tell me…? or Do you know…? followed by statement word order, not question word order.

Example: Could you tell me where the library is?

5. Present modals expressing ability

Use can or cannot + base verb for present ability. Be able to is an alternative and can be used in more tenses.

Example: She can manage her schedule independently.

6. Adverbial linkers

Use however to contrast, therefore to show a result, and moreover or in addition to add information. Check punctuation.

Example: The weather was severe; therefore, the trip was postponed.

7. Comparative adjectives

Use -er with many short adjectives and more with longer ones; use than to compare two things. Remember irregular forms such as better and worse.

Example: A shared studio is cheaper than a private flat.

1. Sharing a Home: More Than Splitting Costs 3A · At grade level · 258 words · 8 questions

Sharing a Home: More Than Splitting Costs

Sharing a living space can teach young people lessons that are difficult to learn alone. At first, students often think only about dividing the rent or finding enough room for their belongings. In practice, successful shared living depends on patience, clear expectations and respect for privacy.

At a local secondary school, a student committee interviewed classmates who had shared rooms during school trips. Many reported that a simple schedule prevented disagreements about lights, study time and cleaning. Others said that asking permission before borrowing belongings mattered more than they had expected.

The committee also noticed a less obvious benefit. Students learned to explain their needs without blaming other people. When two roommates preferred different bedtimes, they discussed the problem and agreed on a quiet hour rather than arguing. Their compromise did not remove every difficulty, but it made the room more comfortable for both.

However, sharing does not mean giving up personal boundaries. A respectful roommate understands that a closed notebook, private message or quiet moment should not be interrupted. Written rules can help, but listening carefully is equally important.

The examples also show why evidence must be interpreted carefully. One experience can illustrate a wider issue, but it cannot prove that everyone will respond in the same way. Comparing several viewpoints makes the final judgement more reliable.

The committee concluded that shared living is not automatically enjoyable or stressful. Its success depends on the habits people develop together. Learning to cooperate in a small space may prepare students to work with very different people later in life.

2. When the Weather Changes Our Plans 3B · Beyond grade level · 258 words · 8 questions

When the Weather Changes Our Plans

Weather affects far more than the clothes people choose each morning. Temperature, humidity and sudden storms can change how communities travel, work and stay healthy. Understanding these connections helps people make safer decisions.

During a period of unusually high humidity, an outdoor youth event was moved indoors. Organisers did not cancel it immediately; they checked forecasts, consulted safety guidance and arranged extra drinking water. Their decision showed that planning can reduce risk without ending an activity completely.

Some participants initially felt disappointed. They had imagined spending the afternoon outside, and an indoor hall seemed less exciting. Yet the new location allowed younger children and older visitors to take part comfortably. Volunteers also used the change to explain how the body responds to heat.

A science teacher noted that weather and climate are different concepts. A hot afternoon describes weather, while climate concerns patterns observed over many years. Confusing the two can lead to weak conclusions about environmental change.

The examples also show why evidence must be interpreted carefully. One experience can illustrate a wider issue, but it cannot prove that everyone will respond in the same way. Comparing several viewpoints makes the final judgement more reliable.

For students, the practical lesson is to ask what a claim means, who benefits from it and what information is missing. A careful reader separates an observed fact from a prediction or a personal preference.

The event ended successfully because organisers responded to evidence rather than assumptions. Their experience suggests that flexibility, communication and reliable information are essential when conditions become unpredictable.

3. A Shared Studio: A Practical Experiment 3A · At grade level · 246 words · 8 questions

A Shared Studio: A Practical Experiment

When Mariam and Hessa joined a summer design programme, they were offered a small studio to share for two weeks. The studio had one desk, two beds and a narrow kitchen. At first, both girls expected the arrangement to be simple because they were close friends. By the third day, however, they had discovered that friendship did not automatically solve practical problems.

Mariam preferred to sketch early in the morning, while Hessa worked best after dinner. Their first disagreement concerned the desk. Instead of asking the programme supervisor to intervene, they created a timetable and agreed that either student could request an occasional change. They also decided to keep personal materials in separate boxes.

The most surprising difficulty was noise. Mariam liked listening to music while designing, but Hessa needed silence to concentrate. Headphones helped, although they were not a complete solution. When deadlines approached, both students agreed to limit unnecessary interruptions.

At the end of the programme, the girls evaluated their experience. They had saved space and learned to organise their time, but they also recognised the importance of privacy. Hessa said that sharing a studio worked because they could discuss problems before frustration grew. Mariam added that the arrangement would have been harder without clear rules.

Their experience suggests that a shared studio can support cooperation, provided that both people accept responsibility. The important question is not simply whether the room is large enough, but whether its occupants can respect different habits and needs.

4. A Heatwave Changes the School Day 3B · Above grade level · 253 words · 8 questions

A Heatwave Changes the School Day

During an unusually hot week, a coastal school changed its outdoor activity schedule. Morning temperatures rose quickly, and high humidity made the air feel warmer than the thermometer suggested. The school had planned a gardening project, but teachers decided that student safety should come first.

Instead of cancelling the project completely, the science teacher divided it into shorter tasks. Students examined plants indoors, recorded temperature readings and compared shaded areas with spaces exposed to direct sunlight. A small group watered the garden early, when conditions were cooler, while the rest prepared information posters.

One student, Alia, was surprised that two locations with similar temperatures could feel so different. Her teacher explained that humidity affects how efficiently sweat evaporates from the skin. When the air already contains a great deal of moisture, the body may struggle to cool itself. Students were reminded that drinking water and taking breaks were sensible precautions, not signs of weakness.

The class also considered how buildings could reduce heat. Trees offered shade, light-coloured roofs reflected more sunlight, and covered walkways made movement between rooms more comfortable. However, the teacher warned that no single solution would remove every risk during severe weather.

By Friday, the students had completed their posters and collected useful observations without spending long periods outdoors. The change of plan had not weakened the project; it had given students a chance to investigate the weather directly. They concluded that adapting activities to conditions is a practical skill, especially in places where extreme heat can affect daily routines.

5. Which feature helps by reflecting sunlight?
6. What can be inferred about the teacher’s approach?

Topic 1: Sharing a Studio: Is It a Good Living Arrangement for Students?

Three guiding questions
  1. What benefits can students gain from sharing a small studio?
  2. What difficulties might arise with privacy, noise and shared responsibilities?
  3. Do you think sharing a studio is a good choice for students, and why?
Study the full annotated model essay

Introduction

[Hook] Imagine coming home after a long school day and finding that your study area is also someone else’s living space. [Background information] A studio is usually one main room that combines sleeping, studying and living areas, often with a separate bathroom. Sharing one can save money, but it also requires patience and clear agreements. [Thesis statement] I believe students can benefit from sharing a studio if they respect one another’s privacy, divide responsibilities fairly and agree on clear rules.

Body Paragraph 1

[Topic sentence] The strongest advantage of sharing a studio is that students can reduce costs and support one another. [Supporting detail] Rent and some household expenses can be divided, leaving more money for books, transport and other necessities. [Example / evidence] For example, two students might share the cost of internet access and basic furniture instead of paying for everything separately. [Supporting detail] Living together can also encourage cooperation and help students develop independence.

Body Paragraph 2

[Topic sentence] However, limited space can make privacy and concentration difficult. [Supporting detail] One student may need silence to study while the other wants to make a phone call or rest. [Example / evidence] For instance, a student preparing for an exam might be disturbed by a roommate watching videos nearby. [Supporting detail] A shared schedule and agreed quiet hours can reduce these conflicts.

Body Paragraph 3

[Topic sentence] Some people argue that students should never share a studio because it is too crowded. [Supporting detail] Their concern is reasonable because personal space is important for wellbeing and effective study. [Example / evidence] Nevertheless, students who set boundaries, organise storage and communicate respectfully can manage a small space more successfully. [Supporting detail] Therefore, the success of this arrangement depends more on cooperation and suitability than on space alone.

Conclusion

[Restated thesis] Overall, sharing a studio can be a positive choice for students who are prepared to cooperate. [Summary of main points] It may save money and build responsibility, although privacy and noise must be managed carefully. [Final thought] Before moving in, students should discuss expectations honestly and decide whether the arrangement suits them.

Sharing a Studio — Summary practice 1 179 words

Sharing a small studio can help students manage living costs and learn to cooperate. When two people divide rent, electricity and internet bills, each may spend less than someone living alone. They can also share responsibilities such as cleaning the kitchen and buying basic supplies. However, a studio usually has only one main room, so privacy can be difficult. One student may need silence to revise while the other wants to make a phone call. Without clear agreements, small disagreements can grow into serious arguments. Students who share successfully often discuss their routines before moving in. They decide when visitors are welcome, where belongings should be kept and how expenses will be divided. They also respect each other’s study time and personal space. Sharing a studio is therefore neither automatically convenient nor necessarily uncomfortable. Its success depends on communication, realistic expectations and the willingness of both students to compromise. A written schedule can make responsibilities visible and prevent one person from doing all the work. If a problem continues, a calm conversation is usually more helpful than ignoring it.

Living Together or Alone — Summary practice 2 173 words

Young adults often face a choice between living with others and renting a place alone. Shared accommodation can reduce costs because residents divide bills and household expenses. It can also provide companionship, particularly for students who have recently moved away from their families. A housemate may offer help during a difficult week or remind someone about an important deadline. Living alone offers different advantages. People can arrange their own schedules, study without interruptions and make decisions about visitors or noise. Yet independence may bring higher costs and a greater responsibility for cleaning, shopping and maintenance. Neither arrangement suits everyone. A student with a limited budget may value shared accommodation, while someone who works unusual hours may prefer more privacy. Before deciding, young adults should consider their finances, habits and need for quiet. They should also think about travel time to college or work. A comfortable home is not simply the cheapest option; it is one that supports daily routines and well-being. Honest discussion can help people choose an arrangement that fits their circumstances.

Preparing for Severe Weather — Summary practice 3 176 words

Severe weather can disrupt ordinary routines with little warning. Heavy rain may flood roads, while strong winds can damage outdoor objects and make travel unsafe. Very high temperatures also create risks, especially for people who spend long periods outside. Schools can help students respond by teaching them how to read official warnings and recognise dangerous conditions. Practical drills show students where to shelter and how to contact an adult in an emergency. Families have an important role as well. They can prepare water, essential supplies and a list of emergency telephone numbers. Some people believe that weather education takes time away from other subjects. However, supporters argue that even a short lesson can prevent confusion when conditions change suddenly. Good preparation does not mean students should feel frightened. Instead, it gives them simple actions they can remember under pressure. Teachers should also explain that advice differs according to the type of weather. For example, staying indoors during a thunderstorm is different from keeping cool during a heatwave. Reliable information and calm decisions help communities remain safe.
